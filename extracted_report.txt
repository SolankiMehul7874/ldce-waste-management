Chapter 1 
INTRODUCTION	
 
Solid waste management represents one of the most critical civic, ecological, and institutional 
challenges confronting modern educational campuses across urban India. With rapid urban 
development, expanding academic infrastructure, and swelling student enrollments, educational 
institutions generate complex waste streams comprising paper, single-use food packaging, plastics, 
aluminium cans, botanical foliage, and cafeteria biodegradable scraps. In the absence of an organized 
source-segregation architecture, these diverse waste fractions become commingled at the point of 
disposal, transforming high-value recyclable commodities and nutrient-rich organic biomass into 
putrescible, contaminated refuse that ends up overburdening municipal dumpsites.	
L. D. College of Engineering (LDCE), Ahmedabad, established in 1948 and affiliated with the 
Directorate of Technical Education, Gujarat State, spans a vibrant 59-acre campus hosting over 6,000 
students, faculty, and administrative personnel across 14 engineering departments, laboratories, 
canteens, workshops, and student hostels. As a premier technological institution, LDCE has both the 
civic imperative and technical capability to pioneer sustainable, circular campus waste management 
models.	
This micro-project, entitled “Design and Implementation of a Low -Cost Sustainable Solid Waste 
Segregation and Recycling System for Campus Waste Management”, has been conceptualized, 
planned, and executed by B.E. Semester V Information Technology students as an experiential 
project management exercise. The overarching goal of the project is twofold:	
• Technical Objective: To engineer and pilot-test a low-cost, decentralized solid waste segregation 
and recycling station utilizing repurposed materials, paired with an aerobic composting pilot and 
a digital waste audit log.	
• Management Objective: To rigorously apply modern project management methodologies —
including Project Charter formulation, Work Breakdown Structure (WBS), Gantt scheduling, 
RACI responsibility assignment, budget variance estimation, risk mitigation, and quality 
assurance—within an academic 8-week lifecycle and an austere budget ceiling of ₹1,700.	
By bridging environmental engineering with information technology and formal project management, 
this initiative provides a scalable, empirical blueprint that transitions the college from passive, 
commingled waste disposal to an active, zero-waste circular campus model.

Chapter 2 
BACKGROUND AND NEED FOR THE PROJECT	
 
Rapid urbanization and institutional expansion in India have triggered an alarming escalation in the 
quantum and heterogeneity of municipal solid waste. Higher educational campuses, characterized by 
dense, transient student populations, extensive administrative operations, laboratory complexes, and 
food service centers, represent miniature municipalities. Nationally, the Ministry of Environment, 
Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC), Government of India, promulgated the landmark Solid Waste 
Management (SWM) Rules, 2016, which statutorily mandate all waste generators —including 
academic institutions, commercial establishments, and bulk waste generators—to segregate waste at 
source into three primary streams: biodegradable (wet), recyclable (dry), and domestic hazardous.	
In Gujarat, the Gujarat Pollution Control Board (GPCB) and the Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation 
(AMC) have instituted stringent municipal bye-laws enforcing decentralized waste segregation under 
the national Swachh Bharat Mission (Urban) and Mission LiFE (Lifestyle for Environment). The city 
of Ahmedabad generates approximately 4,000 tonnes of municipal solid waste per day. Historically, a 
substantial portion of this unsegregated waste has accumulated at legacy dumpsites, notably the 
Pirana landfill, causing severe environmental degradation, subsurface groundwater contamination via 
toxic leachate, and uncontrolled methane emissions triggering spontaneous landfill fires.	
A baseline reconnaissance survey conducted across L. D. College of Engineering revealed several 
compelling operational realities that establish the urgent need for this project:	
• 1. Ubiquitous Commingled Disposal: Most departmental corridors, computer laboratories, and 
common areas are equipped with single, unsegregated open dustbins. Students and staff 
inadvertently dispose of half-eaten snacks, wet tea cups, paper assignments, plastic wraps, and 
discarded batteries into the same receptacle.	
• 2. Value Destruction of Recyclables: When recyclable paper and clean cardboard come into 
contact with wet food scraps and sugary tea residues, the cellulose fibers degrade and become 
biologically contaminated, rendering hundreds of kilograms of high-grade paper unrecyclable and 
fit only for landfilling.	
• 3. Sanitation and Public Health Risks: Open bins and unmanaged corner disposal points attract 
stray dogs, cats, rodents, and flies, creating persistent foul odors, visual blight, and potential 
hygienic hazards in high-footfall academic zones.	
• 4. Behavioral Bottleneck: Despite high theoretical environmental awareness, the student body 
lacks clear visual cues, standardized color-coded receptacles, and hands-free hygienic disposal 
mechanisms at the immediate point of consumption.	
• 5. High Capital Cost of Commercial Bins: Commercial segregation kiosks available from 
industrial vendors cost between ₹15,000 and ₹45,000 per unit, which is financially prohibitive for 
wide-scale collegiate adoption. Hence, there is a pressing need for a frugal, low-cost engineering 
solution (< ₹1,700) built from locally upcycled campus materials.	
By tackling these five root causes, this micro -project demonstrates how disciplined project 
management, value-engineering, and student participation can convert an institutional waste liability 
into an ecological asset.

PROBLEM STATEMENT	
 
The formal problem statement addressed by this Project Management Micro-Project is formulated as 
follows:	
KEY CONCEPT / FORMULA: FORMAL PROBLEM STATEMENT 
“Educational facilities at L. D. College of Engineering generate substantial daily volumes of mixed solid waste 
that are currently collected in single, unsegregated bins. This lack of source segregation causes high rates of 
recyclable material contamination (>80%), eliminates on-site organic composting potential, produces corridor 
unhygienic conditions, and results in 100% of generated waste being routed to municipal landfills. 
Furthermore, the absence of quantitative waste tracking and high commercial bin costs prevent effective 
administrative intervention. There is an urgent need to engineer, pilot-test, and evaluate a low-cost (< ₹1,700), 
upcycled, multi-stream segregation station paired with a digital monitoring audit system to divert at least 80% 
of pilot waste from landfills.”	
	
To diagnose the systemic root causes leading to this problem, a Project Management Ishikawa 
(Fishbone) Cause-and-Effect Analysis was conducted across six dimensions:	
• 1. Infrastructure / Equipment: Absence of multi -compartment color-coded receptacles; rusted 
single bins without foot pedals; lack of outdoor rain/sun protective canopies.	
• 2. Process / Methods: Absence of a standard operating procedure (SOP) for campus housekeeping 
to maintain segregated collection; no scheduled tie-up with authorized AMC recyclers.	
• 3. Materials / Waste Streams: Rapid discard of single-use food packaging, disposable cold drink 
PET bottles, and printed rough sheets during semester exams and laboratory submissions.	
• 4. People / Behavioral: High general awareness among engineering students but poor disposal 
discipline; lack of immediate peer nudges; absence of Gujarati/Hindi visual infographics.	
• 5. Measurement / Monitoring: Zero quantitative data on daily campus waste generation rates, 
composition percentages, or recyclable recovery value.	
• 6. Environment / Climate: Ahmedabad's high ambient temperature (>40°C in summer) 
accelerating food waste fermentation and odor generation in unsegregated bins.

Chapter 7 
PROPOSED SUSTAINABLE WASTE MANAGEMENT SYSTEM	
 
The proposed system establishes a low -cost, decentralized, circular solid waste segregation and 
recycling architecture engineered specifically for the operational dynamics of an engineering college 
campus. The core philosophy of the system is source segregation—preventing waste fractions from 
ever mingling, thereby preserving the economic value of recyclables and the biological purity of 
organic compostable matter.	
	
Figure 2.1: Integrated Campus Solid Waste Management Lifecycle Flowchart	
7.1 Four-Stream Segregation Protocol	
In strict compliance with the Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016, and CPCB institutional 
guidelines, waste on campus is classified into four distinct streams, each assigned a standardized 
color code, aperture geometry, and visual infographic:	


=== PAGE 13 ===

Figure 3.1: Campus Waste Segregation Protocol & Classification Guide	
• 1. Green Stream (Wet / Biodegradable Waste): Dedicated exclusively to organic, putrescible 
matter generated primarily from cafeteria leftovers, tiffin boxes, fruit peels, tea bags, and fallen 
campus garden foliage. The collected organic waste is transferred daily to the campus aerobic 
composting unit. Key Rule: No plastic bags, foils, or non-biodegradable wrappers.	
• 2. Blue Stream (Clean Dry Recyclables): Dedicated to high -grade recyclable commodities 
including paper printouts, notebooks, exam sheets, flattened corrugated cardboard cartons, rinsed 
PET beverage bottles, and aluminium soft-drink cans. Key Rule: All beverage containers must be 
emptied and crushed before deposition to optimize volumetric capacity.	
• 3. Red / Yellow Stream (Domestic Hazardous & E -Waste): A specialized secure sub-stream 
positioned primarily in hardware laboratories and departmental staff rooms to capture dry cell 
batteries, printer toners, spent fluorescent/LED lamps, broken lab glassware, and sanitary waste 
wrapped in paper. Key Rule: Prevents heavy metals and bio -contaminants from polluting 
common recycling streams.	
• 4. Black Stream (Non-Recyclable Rejects / Inerts): Reserved strictly for contaminated, non -
recyclable residual inerts such as multi-layered metallized snack packets (chips wrappers), wax-
coated papers, soiled sweeping dust, and broken ceramics. This fraction represents the sole stream 
designated for Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation (AMC) landfill collection.

7.3 In-Situ Aerobic Composting Mechanism	
For the biodegradable organic fraction, the system employs decentralized aerobic composting. A pilot 
20-litre perforated composting unit was prepared with aeration holes (8 mm diameter drilled 
circumferentially) and an internal mesh drainage base to collect excess liquid leachate. Composting 
operates on the principle of microbial decomposition maintained at an optimal Carbon -to-Nitrogen 
(C:N) ratio of approximately 25:1 to 30:1:	
• Nitrogen-rich 'Green' inputs: Cafeteria vegetable peels, fruit rinds, leftover food (moisture: ~65%, 
high N).	
• Carbon-rich 'Brown' bulking agents: Crushed dry neem/gulmohar leaves collected from campus 
lawns and shredded unbleached cardboard packaging (provides structural porosity and absorbs 
excess moisture).	
• Microbial Inoculum: A starter spray of diluted sour buttermilk (chaas) and local fertile soil rich in 
indigenous composting bacteria and actinomycetes was added to accelerate mesophilic 
decomposition without foul odor.

Chapter 9 
PROJECT DESIGN AND WORKING METHODOLOGY	
 
The project design embodies human-centered design (ergonomics), behavioral psychology (nudging 
theory), and hygienic engineering. To ensure wide adoption among engineering students and faculty, 
the station layout addresses common friction points identified during our initial campus survey.	
	
Figure 4.1: Schematic Architectural Layout of Modular Campus Segregation Kiosk	
9.1 Ergonomic and Physical Engineering Specifications	
• 1. Optimal Aperture Height: Bin rim height is positioned at exactly 850 mm from ground level, 
allowing effortless waste deposition for individuals ranging from 150 cm to 185 cm in height 
without bending.	
• 2. Hands-Free Foot-Pedal Mechanism: To overcome student hesitation regarding touching 
potentially dirty bin lids, each bin is actuated by a mechanical foot pedal. Depressing the foot 
pedal raises the lid through a lever linkage; releasing it automatically drops the lid via gravity and 
return spring.	
• 3. Visual Nudge Signage: Mounted at an eye -level elevation of 1.5 meters, a bright yellow 
canopy display features high-contrast text and realistic item illustrations in both English and 
Gujarati, removing any cognitive ambiguity at the moment of discard.	
• 4. Modular Base Elevation: The station is elevated 150 mm on a timber/slotted angle iron skid 
with anti-slip rubber pads, protecting the bins from waterlogging during monsoon rains and 
simplifying corridor floor sweeping.

9.2 Working Methodology: The PDCA Quality Cycle	
The technical execution follows the classical Plan -Do-Check-Act (PDCA) quality framework 
advocated by Edwards Deming, ensuring structured continuous improvement throughout the project 
lifecycle:	
• Phase 1: PLAN (Weeks 1–3): Conducted stakeholder interviews with campus administration, 
housekeeping supervisors, and students. Carried out a 3-day baseline waste characterization audit 
to estimate daily volumes and contamination rates. Formulated project charter and technical 
specifications.	
• Phase 2: DO (Weeks 4–6): Salvaged discarded 20L paint drums from campus stores. Fabricated 
the physical 3-stream pilot station, applied multi-coat enamel finishes, attached foot pedals, and 
installed bilingual signage. Deployed the station in the high-footfall ground floor corridor of the 
IT Department.	
• Phase 3: CHECK (Week 7): Conducted a rigorous 14-day daily waste audit. Weighed each stream 
gravimetrically every morning at 10:00 AM using the calibrated digital scale. Logged data into 
the cloud-based Google Sheets audit tracker. Monitored contamination instances and composter 
temperature/moisture.	
• Phase 4: ACT (Week 8): Analyzed audit logs, calculated key performance indicators (Landfill 
Diversion Rate, Segregation Accuracy %), adjusted compost bulking ratios to eliminate moisture, 
and established a formalized recyclable collection channel with an authorized AMC scrap partner.

STEP-BY-STEP IMPLEMENTATION / CONSTRUCTION 
PROCESS	
 
The physical fabrication of the pilot waste segregation station was executed systematically by the 
student project team in the college workshop over an intensive 10-day period. The entire construction 
process was documented to ensure seamless reproducibility by other departments across LDCE.	
	
Figure 5.1: Engineering Schematic of Low-Cost Prototype Bin Construction	
Phase 1: Salvage, Degreasing, and Mechanical Preparation	
Three discarded 20-litre commercial plastic paint drums were retrieved from the campus maintenance 
storage yard. The containers were subjected to a rigorous multi-stage cleaning process: scraped free 
of residual dried paint using putty knives, degreased with warm detergent solution, and high-pressure 
water washed. After sun drying, eight 6-mm ventilation holes were drilled around the top rim of the 
wet waste bin to prevent anaerobic gas buildup.	
Phase 2: Surface Abrasion and Dual-Coat Enamel Application	
Because untreated high-density polyethylene (HDPE) plastic has low surface energy, the outer 
surfaces were abraded uniformly using 120-grit emery sandpaper to create mechanical anchoring 
profiles. A single base coat of synthetic primer was applied and cured for 12 hours. Next, two heavy 
coats of quick-drying synthetic enamel were applied:	
• Forest Green Enamel: Wet / Biodegradable Waste Receptacle (Coded according to SWM Rules 
2016).	
• Royal Blue Enamel: Clean Dry Recyclable Waste Receptacle.	
• Matte Jet Black Enamel: Non-Recyclable Rejects / Landfill Receptacle.

collection protocols, separate bag handling, and digital weighing procedures.

Chapter 17 
SAFETY AND ENVIRONMENTAL CONSIDERATIONS	
 
Solid waste management projects involve inherent biological, chemical, and ergonomic hazards that 
necessitate stringent occupational health and safety protocols alongside environmental protection 
standards.	
17.1 Occupational Health & Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)	
Waste handling carries risks of lacerations from broken glass or staples, contact with pathogenic 
microorganisms, and inhalation of dust or fungal spores. The team instituted the following non -
negotiable safety protocols:	
• 1. Mandatory Puncture-Resistant Gloves: All team members and housekeeping staff must wear 
heavy-duty puncture-resistant nitrile gloves during waste collection, segregation auditing, and 
weighing. Disposable medical latex gloves are strictly prohibited due to tear vulnerability.	
• 2. Respiratory Protection: N95 particulate respirators must be worn during composter turning and 
gravimetric waste sorting to filter airborne bio-aerosols, dust, and microbial spores.	
• 3. Hygiene & Hand Sanitation: Strict prohibition of open hand sorting. Hand tongs or blunt 
scrapers must be used when separating commingled items during daily audits. Hands must be 
washed with antibacterial soap immediately following activities.	
17.2 Environmental Safeguards & Vector Management	
• 1. Leachate Containment: The pilot composter is elevated on a concrete catch tray lined with 
sawdust to capture any incidental leachate, preventing liquid runoff into campus drainage or soil.	
• 2. Vector & Vermin Control: To prevent mosquitoes, houseflies, and rodents from colonizing 
bins, all lids feature airtight lip seals, and the wet bin is cleared daily by 4:00 PM prior to evening 
building closure.	
• 3. Zero Open Burning: Strict adherence to Solid Waste Management Rules 2016 Section 15(g), 
completely prohibiting the open burning of waste, fallen garden foliage, or plastic packaging 
anywhere on college grounds.

Chapter 18 
ADVANTAGES AND ENVIRONMENTAL BENEFITS	
 
The implementation of a decentralized solid waste segregation and recycling system on an 
engineering campus yields quantifiable ecological, financial, and pedagogical advantages.	
18.1 Quantitative Environmental Impact Metrics	
• 1. High Landfill Diversion Rate (88.5%): During the 14 -day pilot audit, the single station 
collected an average of 7.8 kg/day of waste. By separating 4.1 kg/day of wet waste for 
composting and 2.8 kg/day of dry recyclables, exactly 6.9 kg/day was diverted away from 
municipal dumpsites—achieving an empirical Landfill Diversion Rate of 88.5%.	
• 2. Greenhouse Gas Abatement: When organic waste decomposes anaerobically in unsegregated 
landfills, it emits methane (CH4), a potent greenhouse gas with a global warming potential 28 
times greater than CO2. Aerobic composting of 4.1 kg/day of food waste prevents approx. 4.9 kg 
of CO2-equivalent emissions daily (approx. 980 kg CO2e over an academic year per station).	
• 3. Natural Resource Conservation: Every 54 kg of paper collected and sent for recycling 
conserves approximately one mature tree, 1,300 litres of water, and 160 kWh of electrical energy 
compared to manufacturing virgin paper pulp.	
• 4. In-Situ Soil Conditioning: The pilot composter converts food scraps into nutrient -dense, 
stabilized organic humus, returning vital nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (NPK) to campus 
garden beds without chemical fertilizers.	
18.2 Economic and Institutional Benefits	
• 1. Frugal Capital Expenditure: Reclaiming 20L commercial paint drums and scrap timber 
produced a fully functional station for ₹1,670 INR—delivering a 91% cost saving compared to 
commercial fiberglass bins priced at ₹18,500.	
• 2. Recyclable Monetization: Clean segregated paper, cardboard, and PET bottles command 
premium scrap market rates (₹12–15/kg for paper, ₹22–28/kg for PET). Accumulated funds can 
be channeled directly into the student Green Club to sponsor campus tree plantation.	
• 3. Institutional Accreditations: Elevates L. D. College of Engineering's institutional rating under 
Swachh Campus national rankings, NAAC criteria, and National Institutional Ranking 
Framework (NIRF) sustainability parameters.

Chapter 19 
LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE IMPROVEMENTS	
 
In accordance with rigorous academic scholarship, the limitations encountered during the pilot 
execution are candidly analyzed alongside a phased roadmap for future enhancement.	
19.1 Observed Project Limitations	
• 1. Limited Volumetric Capacity: The 20-litre bucket capacity requires daily clearing; during high-
intensity events (such as department symposiums or exams), the blue recyclable bin experiences 
rapid volumetric overflow from large cardboard boxes.	
• 2. Manual Data Entry: Daily logging relies on manual weighing using a handheld digital scale 
and Google Forms data entry, creating vulnerability to human omission or recording delays.	
• 3. Behavioral Friction: While student compliance reached 92% by Day 14, initial days exhibited 
contamination from greasy food wrappers tossed into the blue paper bin, indicating that 
behavioral inertia requires persistent nudges.	
• 4. Outdoor Weather Protection: The prototype was deployed in an indoor hallway corridor; 
outdoor deployment would require enhanced UV-stabilized polycarbonate roofing and anchor 
bolting against monsoon gusts.	
19.2 Roadmap for Future Technical Improvements	
• 1. IoT-Enabled Fill-Level Telemetry: Integrate an ultrasonic distance sensor (HC -SR04) and 
ESP32 microcontroller inside each bin lid to measure bin fill level in real time. When the bin 
reaches 80% capacity, an automated alert is triggered via Wi-Fi to campus housekeeping staff.	
• 2. Gamified Student Mobile App: Develop a cross-platform mobile application ('Green LDCE') 
allowing students to scan bin QR codes when disposing of clean recyclables to earn academic 
green credits redeemable for library perks or canteen discounts.	
• 3. Solar-Powered Rotary Composter: Transition from the 5L pilot compost bin to a centralized 
100-litre dual-chamber rotating composting drum installed near the campus botanical garden, 
powered by a small solar PV aeration fan.

Chapter 21 
CONCLUSION	
 
This Project Management Micro-Project successfully demonstrates that sustainable solid waste 
management on a large engineering college campus does not require capital-intensive machinery or 
exorbitant expenditure. By applying core project management methodologies —structured Work 
Breakdown Structures, rigorous Gantt scheduling, unambiguous RACI matrices, proactive risk 
registers, and disciplined cost control —a 5-member student team engineered, fabricated, and 
deployed an effective 3-stream waste segregation station within 8 weeks and under a budget of 
₹1,670 INR.	
The empirical findings from our 14-day pilot deployment in the IT Department corridor validate the 
system's operational efficacy. The station diverted 88.5% of generated waste away from municipal 
landfills, separating 4.1 kg/day of wet organic matter for composting and 2.8 kg/day of clean dry 
recyclables for authorized reprocessing. Student segregation accuracy climbed from an initial 54% to 
92% through clear bilingual visual signage, foot -pedal hands-free hygiene, and digital audit 
monitoring.	
The project stands as a reproducible, low-cost model that can be scaled across all 14 engineering 
departments at L. D. College of Engineering and adapted by academic institutions across Gujarat. In 
doing so, it embodies the spirit of the Swachh Bharat Mission, Mission LiFE, and statutory Solid 
Waste Management Rules 2016, proving that engineering students can lead the transition toward 
circular, zero-waste campuses.
